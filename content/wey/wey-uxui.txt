[TEXT]
Heading: Which wey?
Body:
Wey is a mobility platform focused on helping commuters navigate city transportation more affordably and predictably. 

It combines route planning, real-time crowdsourced intelligence, and a pay-later transport card that offers users discounts at the end of the month.
[/TEXT]
[/GRID]

[TEXT: pullQuote]
Body: Lagos commuters travelling to unfamiliar destinations struggle to determine the most practical combination of transportation options, often relying on multiple sources of information and other people to navigate their journey.
[/TEXT]

[TEXT]
Body: Navigating Lagos can be challenging, particularly when travelling to an unfamiliar part of the city. While mapping services can provide directions, getting around Lagos often involves a combination of buses, BRT, Danfo, Keke, walking, and other forms of transportation.
[/TEXT]

[TEXT]
Heading: How do Lagos commuters move?
Body: To understand how Lagos commuters plan journeys to unfamiliar destinations or to any destination at all, how they choose between different routes, what challenges they encounter, and how they currently solve those challenges. To answer these questions, I had to ask a few questions: 
1. How frequently do Lagos commuters travel to unfamiliar areas?
2. How do they currently figure out how to get there?
3. What makes choosing a route difficult?
4. What happens when they choose the wrong or inefficient route?
5. What information would make unfamiliar journeys easier?

Participants were between 15 and 35 years old and regularly moved around Lagos using public and private transportation. 12 participants took part in a semi-structured interview.

I conducted a broader survey with 56 Lagos residents to identify whether patterns observed during the interviews were common across a larger group.

Participants included students, full-time employees, and freelancers living in areas including Lagos Island, Yaba, Surulere, Ikeja, Lekki, Ajah, and mainland communities.
[/TEXT]

[TEXT]
Heading: Key findings
Body: 1. Travelling to unfamiliar areas is common.
2. People rarely rely on one source of information.
3. Fastest is not always best.
4. People rely heavily on other people.

Before leaving, most used Google Maps to locate their destination and understand what area it was in, following a simple process of *Where is it?* → *What area is it in?* → *How do I get there?*

During the journey, especially at bus stops or in unfamiliar neighbourhoods, participants often switched from digital tools to asking drivers, conductors, or other passengers questions such as *"Where can I get a bus going to...?"*, *"Does this bus pass through...?"*, or *“Where do I stop?"* This shows that route discovery isn't a one-time activity that happens before a journey, but an ongoing process that continues as commuters navigate unfamiliar routes and locations.
[/TEXT]

[TEXT]
Heading: The gap
Body: This presents an opportunity to create a journey-planning experience designed specifically around the realities of moving through Lagos. Instead of simply answering “Where is this place?", the experience should help commuters understand “What exactly should I do to get there?” by providing practical information such as 
1. Which transport to take
2. Where to board, where to change, where to get off
3. Approximate journey time 
4. Estimated cost 
5. Number of transfers 
6. Walking distance 
7. Alternative routes 
8. Current route conditions 

Rather than presenting one universally “best” route, the experience could offer options based on what matters most to the commuter, such as 

*Cheapest, Fastest, Simplest, or Fewest transfers.*
[/TEXT]

[TEXT]
Heading: Multi-leg trips
Body: Where to board, where to change, where to get off — according to the data, it's clear most commuters typically make multi-leg trips. 

When a user inputs a destination that requires a transfer, the system automatically breaks the trip into two connected routes, clearly showing where to get off, where to board next, the estimated time, and the cost of each trip, as well as the total projected fare.
[/TEXT]

[TEXT]
Heading: User Gets Recommended Routes
Body: Leveraging the multi-leg logic, the system determines whether a second leg of the journey is needed and provides a comprehensive map of the entire trip.

Alternative routes, Approximate journey time, estimated cost, Number of transfers, and Walking distance are available to the user. Users can then choose which option fits there need at the present.

Users who prioritize convenience can check out third-party rides. 
[/TEXT]

[TEXT]
Heading: Crowdsourced updates
Body: According to findings, people rely heavily on other people for information/context about current route conditions, traffic status, and safety.

For each route, users can post real-time information about that specific route, including updating fares when there is a price change, and each post is collated in a timeline linked to that route. 
[/TEXT]

[TEXT]
Heading: Challenges
Body: Most Lagos commuters plan their trips in what they often refer to as *blended commutes.* They start with public transport and end with a ride-hailing service, in a bid to save money and afford some sort of convenience. Designing the multi-leg logic to mirror this real-life scenario, fitting all that information on a page and not overwhelming the user, was a challenge
[/TEXT]
