[TEXT]
Heading: Good food, better distribution.
Body:
Buy and Bite exists to connect the best farm produce and consumers. Rooted in
the belief that the right to be healthy is fundamental, the platform
challenges a system where people are priced out of the very things
that sustain life.

Buy and Bite ensures that affordability and quality are no longer
trade-offs, but standards. I decided to find out why farm produce prices in Lagos, Nigeria, were inflated, what kept people from accessing healthy products, and built a system to solve it.
[/TEXT]
[/GRID]

[TEXT]
Heading: Lagosians can't access or afford farm produce
Body: Lagos Island has its own local markets — Sandgrouse and Sura - but they occupy a specific middle position: Sura Market serves residents in Ikoyi, VI, and nearby areas with fruits, vegetables, grains, and fish at moderate prices, cheaper than retail stores on the Island but slightly higher than the mainland wholesale hubs. Similarly, Sandgrouse Market in the heart of Lagos Island is known for fresh vegetables, fruits, grains, and dairy, and getting there early means the freshest produce before bulk buyers clear it out.

The actually cheap source is elsewhere: Mile 12 is where most traders and restaurants source fresh produce directly from Northern suppliers, with tomatoes, peppers, onions, yams, fruits, and vegetables in huge quantities, and prices are lowest when buying in bulk or early in the morning. It's on the mainland, off the Ikorodu Road axis — a real trip from the Island, not a walk.
[/TEXT]

[TEXT: pullQuote]
Body: Lagos Island residents are paying an "Island premium" for the convenience of not making the mainland trip.
[/TEXT]

[TEXT]
Heading: And this is getting worse
Body: Nigeria's food inflation hit 20.31 percent year-on-year in July 2026, and month-on-month food inflation in July 2026 was highest in Adamawa, Lagos, and Borno among major states — Lagos specifically is running hot even relative to other states.

Structural drivers cited by economists include insecurity on farms, high transportation and logistics costs, expensive energy and fertiliser, supply-chain disruptions, and imported inflation, with food, transportation, housing, utilities and energy together accounting for about 72 percent of current inflationary pressure.
[/TEXT]

[TEXT]
Heading: Where does the money go?
Body: General rule of thumb across markets: retail sits 2–4x above farm-gate price; transport and spoilage account for 10–25% of that gap, with the rest being intermediary margin stacked at each handoff.

In Nigeria specifically, middlemen buy directly from farmers at a discount to market price and transport it to urban markets where they sell at a markup — the discount farmers accept is typically 10-15% below market price, a reasonable trade for speed and cash, though it can be worse.
[/TEXT]

[TEXT]
Body: How much do Lagos Island residents pay in markup for basic staples? What constitutes this markup? Answering these questions would give me the actual Island markup as a hard percentage, not an assumption.

I picked 8 staples and priced them at 3 tiers (Lagos Island retail, Lagos Island's own markets (Sandgrouse, Sura), Mainland wholesale, e.g., Mile 12 or Oyingbo).
[/TEXT]

[TEXT]
Heading: The outcome
Body: Island retail markup over mainland wholesale exceeded ~40–50%, which means the problem is real and sizable (this is a plausible outcome given the 2–4x general retail-vs-farmgate spread cited above)

A meaningful share of residents interviewed say they've skipped or reduced produce purchases due to price, which means demand for a fix is real, not hypothetical

At least 50% of traders were open to bulk/pre-order arrangements, which means a coordination-based fix (group buying, an aggregator run) has a supply-side partner, not just consumer interest
[/TEXT]

[TEXT]
Heading: Turned into insights
Body: I wanted to understand how Lagos Island residents actually approach grocery shopping: what they need, what influences their choices, and what makes the process convenient or inconvenient. For a resident balancing work, school, family, or other responsibilities, going to a market can mean navigating crowded areas, comparing prices across different sellers, carrying purchases, and fitting the trip around an already busy day.

This led to a broader goal: make it easier for people to discover, purchase, and get fresh produce while reducing the time, effort, and uncertainty involved in grocery shopping.

The opportunity for Buy and Bite was therefore to make everyday grocery shopping convenient and more affordable
[/TEXT]

[TEXT]
Heading: Minimum Viable Features
Body: Should this be another mobile app, a WhatsApp feature, an Instagram page, an iMessage feature, a physical warehouse, store, or shop? I asked myself. Going back to the research, users all spoke about a need for one thing — convenience. A mobile app would check the convenience box effectively; everyone uses a mobile app at work, home, and on the go. A mobile app would enable a feature-rich approach, which includes control for users selecting products, stress-free product discovery, a streamlined payment process, and transparency in order tracking.
[/TEXT]

[TEXT]
Heading: People love shopping
Body: I gave users more control over what they buy and how they buy it. Allowing customers to put together products according to their specific or dietary needs rather than being limited to fixed selections was the main reason behind the pick-it-yourself feature. 

To aid users in selection and to reduce cognitive load, products are grouped in categories, e.g., Greens, Nuts, Berries, Veggies, etc.
[/TEXT]

[TEXT]
Heading: Designing for the health-conscious
Body: Buying farm produce was driven by dietary needs for a segment of users, i.e., consumers managing health issues. I translated this into making prepacked packages for specific health issues. 

Users can add or remove products from these prepacked packages to suit their needs or budget. I made the product visuals as clear and descriptive as possible; decision fatigue caused by cognitive load might affect buyers.
[/TEXT]

[TEXT]
Heading: Designing for convenience
Body: Place your order and track it until it's delivered. We know Lagos and its traffic; users will have to spend a whole day to pick up their orders, so I decided to add an option to deliver or schedule the delivery. 

Payment is required before delivery to avoid complications. Multiple payment methods are supported, including cards, third-party payment gateways, and bank transfer.
[/TEXT]

[TEXT]
Heading: Learn. Refine. Iterate.
Body:  Deciding what the payment flow could look like, and what would be optimal for users, was a big challenge. This led to another problem: what is the business model? Does Buy and Bite charge a monthly recurring subscription fee? Pay as you order? Should they have an in-app wallet? 

I had to go back to the buying habits I found in the research and competitive audits to get an answer. Ultimately, I decided the platform should have multiple payment methods, and users should pay as they order.
